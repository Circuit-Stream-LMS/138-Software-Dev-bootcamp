Three ideas before we touch any code

You're about to build a chatbot that answers questions from a company's own documents, and then make it trustworthy enough to actually ship. Here's what each piece does, in plain terms, before we build it for real.

1 — RAG

Giving an AI something to read first

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. It's how a chatbot answers questions about documents it was never trained on.

Think of it like

An AI chatbot, on its own, is like a student taking a test purely from memory. If the question is about something that was never in their textbook, they either say "I don't know" — or they guess, confidently, and get it wrong.

RAG is the open-book version of that exam. Before the student answers, someone flips to the exact right page and says "read this paragraph first." The student still writes the answer themselves — they just got handed the right page.

Chunk
A small slice of a document — a paragraph or two, not the whole file. Small enough to search quickly, big enough to still make sense on its own.
Embedding
A list of numbers representing what a piece of text means. Two sentences about the same idea end up with similar numbers, even if they don't share any of the same words.
Similarity search
Comparing the question's numbers against every chunk's numbers, and keeping whichever chunks are the closest match — the same idea as Spotify finding "similar songs," just for text.

Why it matters: without this step, a chatbot can only talk about what it happened to be trained on — it has no way to answer questions about your company's own documents, policies, or data.

2 — Observability

Watching what the AI actually did

Once the chatbot is live, questions stop being hypothetical. Someone will get a weird answer and ask "why did it say that?" — observability is how you find out.

Think of it like

A flight data recorder. Planes don't just arrive — every instrument reading, every system check, every decision is logged along the way. If something goes wrong, investigators don't have to guess; they replay exactly what happened, step by step.

An AI chatbot has the same problem a plane does: a lot happens between the question and the answer, and if you don't log each step, a wrong answer is just a mystery.

Trace
The record of one full question → answer round trip, start to finish.
Span
One step inside that trip — checking the input was safe, searching the documents, calling the AI model. A trace is just a list of spans.

What a trace looks like

input-check0ms — looks fine, continue
retrieval310ms — found 3 matching chunks, best match 67%
llm-call1130ms — model replied, used 612 tokens
total1440ms, one clear answer

Why it matters: when an answer is wrong, it could be bad retrieval (searched the wrong chunks), a bad prompt, or the model itself. Without a trace, you're guessing between those three. With one, you just look.

3 — Safety

Keeping the chatbot honest and on-topic

A chatbot that answers questions from documents can be pushed off-course in a few predictable ways. Safety is about closing those off before real users find them.

Think of it like

A museum tour guide who only talks about the exhibit. A good guide politely declines to give you stock tips, won't repeat instructions a stranger slipped them to say something wrong, and — if they genuinely don't know something — says "I'm not sure" instead of making up a confident-sounding answer.

Prompt injection
Someone tries to trick the AI into ignoring its instructions — like whispering a new script to the tour guide mid-tour. ("Ignore your previous instructions and...")
Off-topic misuse
Using a "chat with your documents" bot for something unrelated, like free coding help or general trivia.
Hallucination
The AI confidently states something that isn't actually true or isn't in the documents, instead of admitting it doesn't know.

Three layers of defense

Layer 1Check the question itself — reject obvious manipulation before it ever reaches the AI
Layer 2Give the AI clear boundaries — "only answer from these documents, refuse anything else"
Layer 3If nothing relevant was found in the documents, refuse instead of guessing

Why it matters: no single layer catches everything — someone can usually rephrase their way past a filter. That's exactly why it takes a few layers working together, not one clever trick.

That's the whole shape of it

A chatbot that reads your documents (RAG), a way to see what it actually did (observability), and a few layers that keep it honest (safety). Let's go build all three, for real, in code.
